Target ESP32-WROOM-32 DevKit, 38-pin Rows 44–62, straddling centre gap Mirrored pinout · GND at (44, b) LED + buzzer + 5 V motor USB powered · no external pack

Every tie-point for the alert-output stage — a green status LED, a red alert LED, and a transistor-switched buzzer — given as exact (row, column) coordinates you can push parts into without guessing. Built around the pin order printed on your own module, which runs mirrored to the reference layout. Rev B adds the 5 V vibration motor back, switched by a logic-level MOSFET and fed from the board’s own 5V/VIN pin — one supply, one ground, no battery pack.

Confirm the serial port before you wire anything

Unverified · check Device Manager

Two conflicting readings exist for this machine — one says the CP210x driver is bound and the board holds a COM number, the other says Device Manager reports error code 28 on it. Both cannot be true. Open Device Manager → Ports (COM & LPT) and settle it before anything else.

  1. Driver bound → the board shows as Silicon Labs CP210x USB to UART Bridge (COMn) on VID_10C4 / PID_EA60. Use that number in Arduino IDE.
  2. Error code 28 → no driver, therefore no COM port exists at all. Install the Silicon Labs CP210x VCP driver, replug, re-check.
  3. The number is not permanent. Windows assigns it per USB socket — move the cable and it changes. That is why the Python matches on the chip’s VID/PID instead of a hardcoded string.
  4. Never use COM6 here. COM3–COM6 on this machine are all Standard Serial over Bluetooth link. The PORT = "COM6" line in your original notes would open a Bluetooth channel, not the board, and hang without an error.
  5. Only one process can hold the port. Close the Arduino Serial Monitor before running Python, or PySerial raises PermissionError.

Section 02

Where the ESP32 sits

Rows 44 to 62, as specified. That is 19 rows, which is exactly the pin count per side on a 38-pin DevKit — one pin per row, each electrically isolated from its neighbour by the centre gap.

Left header → column b, rows 44–62. Reachable from column a. On your board this side carries GND at row 44, the USB serial pins, and the strapping pins — nothing this circuit uses.

Right header → column i, rows 44–62. Reachable from column j. This is the working side: 3V3 at row 44, GPIO25/26/27/14 at rows 52–55, GND at row 57, and 5V/VIN at row 62 — which is where the motor rail now comes from.

USB connector faces row 63, overhanging the end of the board so the cable clears everything. Columns c, d, e, f, g, h sit underneath the module and are unusable.

Measure the module before you commit. Header-to-header width decides whether this works on one breadboard. Measure centre-to-centre across the two pin rows:

22.9 mm (0.9″) → columns b and i as above, with a and j free. This is the layout drawn here.

25.4 mm (1.0″) → the module lands on a and j and covers every hole in its rows. Push a second breadboard against the first and jumper across, or the pins cannot be reached at all.

Section 04b

Powering the 5 V motor

The motor needs 5 V; the ESP32 needs 3.3 V logic. Rev B resolves that without a second supply, which is what kept Rev A safe.

The 5 V comes from the board, not a pack. Pin 5V/VIN at (62, i) is USB 5 V passed through the DevKit. W13 carries it to the bottom + rail, and W11 feeds the motor from there.

Why that matters. A battery pack reintroduces both hazards Rev A removed: a 5 V node that can back-feed a GPIO, and a supply whose ground floats relative to the board. Sharing USB keeps one supply and one ground, so neither can happen.

No 5 V ever reaches a GPIO. GPIO26 only drives the MOSFET gate, which is isolated from drain and source. The 5 V lives entirely on the + rail, the motor, and Q2’s drain.

Brownout is the real risk now, not damage. Motor inrush sags the 5 V rail. Sag far enough and the ESP32 resets — your alert device reboots at the exact moment it is supposed to be alerting, and nothing on screen tells you it happened.

C1, 100 µF across the bottom rails at position 42, is the fix when you have one. It supplies the inrush locally so the rail does not dip. Stripe leg (−) goes to the − rail.

Symptom to watch for: if the board prints AlertRide ready again in the middle of a DROWSY test, that is a reset, not a glitch.

No capacitor? Build anyway. Nothing is damaged by the sag — the worst case is a reset, which is visible and harmless. C1 is insurance against a failure that may not happen with your motor, not a prerequisite. See the panel below.

Measure your motor’s stall current before you trust this table. The 100 mA figure is typical for a small coin or bar ERM vibration motor. If yours is a geared DC motor, or anything that draws more than about 350 mA stalled, USB cannot feed it alongside the ESP32 and you do need a separate pack — in which case tie its ground to the − rail and never let its + reach a GPIO.

To measure: put a multimeter in series on the 5 V lead, run the motor, then hold the shaft or weight still and read the peak.

If you don’t have a capacitor

Test for the symptom first. Send D, let the motor run ten seconds, and watch the Serial Monitor. No AlertRide ready reprint means the rail is holding and you need nothing at all. Steps 1–7 of bring-up never touch the motor, so none of this blocks you from starting.

If it does reset, work through these in order — cheapest first:

1. Change USB port and cable. A short, thick cable into a rear or motherboard port — never a hub or an unpowered front-panel header. Cable and connector resistance is usually the single biggest contributor to the sag, and this costs nothing to try.

2. Turn on soft-start. Set MOTOR_SOFT_START = true in the sketch. It ramps the motor up over ~25 ms with a software PWM instead of switching it on in one step, spreading the inrush thin enough that the rail usually holds. It deliberately avoids the ESP32’s LEDC peripheral, which tone() is already using for the buzzer, so it cannot break the buzzer stage.

3. Shorten the motor leads, and keep W11 and W13 short.

4. Scavenge a capacitor. Any electrolytic between 47 µF and 1000 µF rated 6.3 V or higher works. They are the small cylinders with a stripe down one side, and they sit in nearly every piece of dead electronics: an old router, a phone charger, a PC power supply, an LED bulb base, an old motherboard. Arduino starter kits usually include a few.

Soft-start is a workaround, not a substitute. Fit a capacitor when you have one and set the flag back to false.

Thirteen wires, still no external supply. W13 makes the bottom + rail a 5 V rail sourced from the board itself. Everything on this breadboard is powered by, and grounded to, the one USB cable.

W12 is the wire that makes both switches work. Q1 compares your GPIO voltage against its own emitter, and Q2 against its own source. Without a shared ground either sees an undefined drive voltage and latches on or never turns on. Fit W12 before any signal wire.

Fit W13 last, after the motor stage is built and tested dry. It is the only wire that puts 5 V on the board; until it goes in, a mistake in the motor stage cannot do anything.

Both rails above column a stay empty. Only the bottom pair is used — − for ground, + for the 5 V motor rail.

Q1 leg order flips between the two parts you may have. Hold it with the flat face toward you and legs pointing down, then read left to right:

BC547 → C B E, so row 21 = collector, 22 = base, 23 = emitter. This is what the map assumes.

2N2222 (TO-92) → E B C, the reverse. If you use this part, swap W6 to (21, j) and the buzzer negative to (23, g).

Base stays at row 22 either way. Confirm against the datasheet for your exact marking before power.

If the buzzer never sounds at all, check W12 first. A missing ground is by far the most common cause, and it looks identical to a dead transistor.

Q2 (IRLZ44N, TO-220) reads G D S left to right with the printed face toward you and the metal tab pointing away, toward row 63. So (33, i) = gate, (34, i) = drain, (35, i) = source. The tab is electrically the drain — do not let it touch anything.

Why the IRLZ44N and not another MOSFET. Its gate threshold is 1–2 V, so a 3.3 V GPIO turns it fully on. A standard IRF540 needs ~10 V and would sit half-on, heating up and running the motor weakly. Check for the L in the part number; it means logic-level.

D1 backwards is the one mistake that kills the build instantly. Reversed, it shorts the 5 V rail to the drain the moment W13 goes in. The banded (cathode) end goes to row 40, the + side. Check it twice.

R5 is not decoration. Between reset and pinMode() GPIO26 floats, and a floating gate can hold enough charge to run the motor. R5 drains it, so the motor is off from the instant power is applied.

Confirm three labels before you wire anything. Two data points fixed this mirror; a third makes it certain. On the right header, row 52 should read GPIO25, row 55 GPIO14, and row 57 GND. If any of those disagree, stop and tell me what is actually printed — wiring into rows 59–62 on either side would reach the flash pins.

All four chosen pins are clean, and row 62 is safe to tap. GPIO14, 25, 26 and 27 have no boot-time strapping duty and no flash connection, so a load on any of them cannot stop the board from starting. Row 62 on the right header is 5V/VIN, not a GPIO at all — taking current out of it is what it is for. Do not confuse it with row 62 on the left header, which is the flash clock. That is why they sit where they do — and why row 56 (GPIO12) stays empty even though it is right in the middle of the cluster: a pull-up there at boot sets the flash to the wrong voltage and the board will not come up.

Section 08

Firmware

Complete sketch. Board in Arduino IDE: ESP32 Dev Module. Upload speed 921600, flash frequency 80 MHz, monitor 115200.

esp32_alert / esp32_alert.inoArduino C++ · Rev B
// AlertRide alert driver - LED + buzzer + 5 V vibration motor (Rev B).
const int GREEN_LED = 14;   // row 55, col i
const int RED_LED   = 27;   // row 54, col i
const int BUZZER    = 25;   // row 52, col i
const int MOTOR     = 26;   // row 53, col i -> Q2 gate

const unsigned long LINK_TIMEOUT_MS = 3000;
const unsigned long MOTOR_PULSE_MS  = 400;

// Leave false unless the board RESETS when the motor kicks in. See
// "If you don't have a capacitor" in Section 04b.
const bool MOTOR_SOFT_START = false;

unsigned long lastCommandAt   = 0;
unsigned long motorPulseUntil = 0;   // 0 = not pulsing
char state = 'S';

// Ramp the motor up to cut inrush. Software PWM at ~3.3 kHz; it avoids
// the LEDC peripheral, which tone() is already using for the buzzer.
void motorOn() {
  if (MOTOR_SOFT_START) {
    for (int duty = 1; duty <= 20; duty++) {
      for (int cycle = 0; cycle < 4; cycle++) {
        digitalWrite(MOTOR, HIGH); delayMicroseconds(duty * 15);
        digitalWrite(MOTOR, LOW);  delayMicroseconds((20 - duty) * 15);
      }
    }
  }
  digitalWrite(MOTOR, HIGH);
}

void allOff() {
  digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, LOW);
  digitalWrite(RED_LED, LOW);
  digitalWrite(MOTOR, LOW);
  motorPulseUntil = 0;
  noTone(BUZZER);
}

void applyState(char c) {
  state = c;

  if (c == 'A') {
    digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, HIGH);
    digitalWrite(RED_LED, LOW);
    digitalWrite(MOTOR, LOW);
    motorPulseUntil = 0;
    noTone(BUZZER);
    Serial.println("AWAKE");
  }
  else if (c == 'W') {
    digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, LOW);
    digitalWrite(RED_LED, HIGH);
    tone(BUZZER, 1800, 250);
    // Pulse, not latch. The 1 Hz keepalive re-triggers it, so WARNING
    // reads as a repeating tap; DROWSY below is the continuous one.
    motorOn();
    motorPulseUntil = millis() + MOTOR_PULSE_MS;
    Serial.println("WARNING");
  }
  else if (c == 'D') {
    digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, LOW);
    tone(BUZZER, 2200);
    motorOn();
    motorPulseUntil = 0;        // latched on until state changes
    Serial.println("DROWSY");
  }
  else if (c == 'S') {
    allOff();
    Serial.println("STOPPED");
  }
}

void setup() {
  // Drive the gate low before anything else. R5 handles the window
  // before this line runs; this holds it for the rest of the session.
  pinMode(MOTOR, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(MOTOR, LOW);

  pinMode(GREEN_LED, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(RED_LED, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(BUZZER, OUTPUT);
  allOff();

  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(200);

  // Boot self-test: each output fires once, so a dead stage shows up
  // here rather than halfway through a demo. The motor goes last and
  // alone - if the board resets during it, the 5 V rail is sagging.
  digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, HIGH); delay(400); digitalWrite(GREEN_LED, LOW);
  digitalWrite(RED_LED, HIGH);   delay(400); digitalWrite(RED_LED, LOW);
  tone(BUZZER, 2000, 200);       delay(400);
  motorOn();                     delay(300); digitalWrite(MOTOR, LOW);

  lastCommandAt = millis();
  Serial.println("AlertRide ready");
}

void loop() {
  if (Serial.available() > 0) {
    char c = Serial.read();
    if (c == '\n' || c == '\r') return;

    lastCommandAt = millis();

    if (c == '?') {
      Serial.println("AlertRide ready");
    } else if (c == 'A' || c == 'W' || c == 'D' || c == 'S') {
      applyState(c);
    }
    return;
  }

  // End a WARNING pulse without blocking the serial read above.
  if (motorPulseUntil != 0 && millis() >= motorPulseUntil) {
    digitalWrite(MOTOR, LOW);
    motorPulseUntil = 0;
  }

  // Red blinks in DROWSY, stays solid in WARNING. Second escalation
  // channel alongside the motor, and the one a camera can see.
  if (state == 'D') {
    digitalWrite(RED_LED, (millis() / 120) % 2 ? HIGH : LOW);
  }

  // Failsafe. A latched motor is worse than a latched buzzer: it can
  // run flat out unattended. Silence from the host stops everything.
  if ((state == 'W' || state == 'D') && millis() - lastCommandAt > LINK_TIMEOUT_MS) {
    allOff();
    state = 'S';
    Serial.println("LINK_LOST");
  }
}

Two motor behaviours, deliberately different. W gives a 400 ms pulse; because the host re-sends the current state once a second as a keepalive, WARNING reads as a repeating tap. D latches the motor on until the state changes. Tap versus continuous is a distinction you feel without looking, which is the whole point of a haptic channel in a driver alert.

The red LED keeps its DROWSY blink. Rev A added it to replace the missing motor; it stays because it is the channel a demo camera can record. Solid red = WARNING, 4 Hz blink = DROWSY.

The failsafe matters more now than it did in Rev A. D latches both a 2200 Hz tone and the motor. If Python crashes or the window closes mid-alert, nothing sends S — and an unattended motor running flat out is a worse failure than a stuck buzzer. Three seconds of host silence clears everything and prints LINK_LOST.

Non-blocking on purpose. The pulse ends via a millis() deadline rather than delay(), so a 400 ms buzz never blocks the serial read — a delay() there would make the failsafe itself unreliable.

If tone() will not compile, your ESP32 Arduino core is older than 3.x, where tone() was added. Either update the core in Boards Manager, or drive the buzzer with ledcWriteTone(). Nothing else in the sketch is core-version sensitive.

Section 10

Bring-up order

Each step adds one stage and proves it before the next goes in. If something fails, you know what caused it.

  1. Settle the COM port per the notice at the top of this sheet. Then select it, plus ESP32 Dev Module, in Arduino IDE.
  2. Seat the ESP32 alone at rows 44–62, columns b and i, USB facing row 63. Confirm GND reads at (44, b) and 3V3 at (44, i) before going further — if they are the other way round, the board is not oriented as this map assumes.
  3. Fit W12 from (57, j) to the bottom − rail. Ground before signals, always.
  4. Upload the sketch over USB with nothing else connected. The boot self-test will do nothing visible yet, but AlertRide ready must appear in the Serial Monitor at 115200.
  5. Build the green LED stage — R1, LED1, W1, W2. Send A. Green lights. If not, check the LED is not backwards before anything else.
  6. Build the red LED stage — R2, LED2, W3, W4. Send W. Green drops, red lights.
  7. Build the buzzer stage — R3, Q1, BZ1, W5, W6, W7. Send W for a short beep, D for a steady tone, S for silence. If it buzzes constantly regardless of command, Q1 is in backwards.
  8. Build the motor stage dry — R4, R5, Q2, D1, M1, W8, W9, W10, W11, and C1 if you have one. Leave W13 out. With no 5 V on the rail nothing can move yet, which is the point: check D1’s band is at row 40, C1’s stripe is on the − rail, and Q2 reads G-D-S into rows 33-34-35.
  9. Fit W13 from (62, j) to the + rail. This is the moment 5 V enters the board. The motor must be still — if it runs the instant you seat this wire, pull it out immediately and check R5 and Q2’s orientation.
  10. Test the motor — send W for a short pulse, D for continuous, S to stop. If the board reprints AlertRide ready when the motor starts, the 5 V rail is browning out — see “If you don’t have a capacitor” in Section 04b.
  11. Run python tests/test_logic.py, then python hardware_test.py --sim. Both run with nothing plugged in. If either fails, the problem is the code, not your wiring — fix it before touching the board.
  12. Close the Serial Monitor and run hardware_test.py. It drives every state for you and prints what the board replies, including the LINK_LOST failsafe. This is the whole hardware check in one command — no camera involved.
  13. Run alertride.py. The dashboard opens at 127.0.0.1:8000. Test in this order: eyes open, one normal blink, a deliberate 1-second closure, a 3-second closure, then cover the camera entirely.
  14. Calibrate the threshold. Watch the on-screen EAR with your eyes open and then shut, and set EAR_THRESHOLD midway between the two. The default 0.25 is a starting point, not a correct value for your face, glasses or lighting.
  15. Log the run — trials, false alarms, missed detections, lighting, glasses on or off. Stationary only, and never in a moving vehicle.
  16. Tape the motor to something. A loose vibration motor walks off the bench and drags the breadboard wires out with it. Tape it to the desk, or to whatever you are demonstrating it against.
